\title{When does an LLM autoresearch loop find real signal?\\
\large A controlled study on twelve biomedical prediction tasks}
\author{\todo{Author Name}\\ \todo{Affiliation}\\ \texttt{\todo{email}}}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Autoresearch loops, in which a large language model (LLM) repeatedly rewrites a single solution
file and a change is kept only if a fixed development score improves, are an increasingly common
way to build predictive pipelines. We ask when such a loop finds signal that holds up on held-out
and external data. We ran a loop driven by Claude Opus 5.5 on 12 biomedical prediction tasks
(scalp and neonatal EEG, resting-state fMRI, 16S microbiome, proteomics and drug response;
3 splits per task, 20 rounds per loop). Each champion was scored once on a held-out 25\% of subjects
and on eight external datasets, and compared with three baselines given the same 20 development
evaluations: one LLM proposal, 20 independent LLM proposals without feedback, and Optuna search
over a hand-written pipeline. Development scores improved in all 36 runs; held-out scores in 27.
Averaged over 11 AUROC tasks the loop gained $+0.052$ AUROC over the seed (95\% CI $+0.009$ to
$+0.095$), compared with $+0.002$ for one proposal, $+0.014$ for 20 proposals without feedback and
$+0.035$ for Optuna. Its advantage over Optuna ($+0.017$, $p=0.29$) was significant only on
abnormal-EEG and neonatal-seizure detection. When site and demographics were available, the loop
built its models on them, and its gains on psychiatric EEG did not transfer to external cohorts.
Feedback is what makes the LLM useful here, but the loop does not replace shortcut-free scoring
and external validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================================
LLM agents can now write, run and revise machine-learning code with little supervision
\citep{huang2024mlagentbench,chan2024mlebench,jiang2025aide,lu2024aiscientist}. The simplest
version is the \emph{autoresearch loop} \citep{karpathy_autoresearch,autoepi}: one editable file,
one fixed metric, and a model that proposes a change each round, kept only if the metric improves.
On development data the loop always looks like it works, because a keep-if-better rule over a
fixed cross-validation (CV) score cannot make that score go down.

Biomedical prediction is a hard test of whether such gains are real. Datasets are small, cases
and controls are often recruited and recorded differently, and site, scanner, batch and
demographics can predict the label better than the biology
\citep{zech2018confounding,geirhos2020shortcut,varoquaux2022failures}. Leakage and shortcuts are
already common in human-written studies \citep{kapoor2023leakage,racz2025szleakage}, and selecting
the best of many candidates on one CV score is itself a source of optimistic bias
\citep{cawley2010overfitting}. An optimizer that tries many ideas against one score has every
opportunity to find all of these.

We therefore ask not whether the loop improves the development score, but \emph{when} the
improvement holds up: on held-out subjects, on external cohorts, and against simpler uses of the
same budget. Across 12 tasks in six domains under one fixed protocol, we contribute
(i)~held-out results for 36 loop runs, each scored once;
(ii)~three same-budget baselines that separate the effect of feedback from that of the LLM and
of ordinary search;
(iii)~multiplicity-corrected and pooled statistics with a sensitivity analysis;
(iv)~evidence that the loop exploits site and demographic shortcuts when they are available; and
(v)~transfer to eight external datasets, run-to-run repeats and a transduction audit.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================================
Benchmarks such as MLAgentBench \citep{huang2024mlagentbench} and MLE-bench \citep{chan2024mlebench}
evaluate agents that write and run training code; AIDE \citep{jiang2025aide} searches code with
tree-structured refinement; the AI Scientist \citep{lu2024aiscientist} automates the wider research
cycle; and FunSearch \citep{romeraparedes2024funsearch} and AlphaEvolve \citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}
pair LLM proposals with an automatic evaluator. Most of this work reports scores on the benchmark
the agent optimized. The autoresearch loop \citep{karpathy_autoresearch} is a deliberately minimal
member of this family, and our protocol reuses that of \citet{autoepi}. As a non-LLM reference we
use tree-structured Parzen estimators \citep{bergstra2011tpe} in Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna}.
Our focus is a controlled evaluation in a domain where selection bias \citep{cawley2010overfitting},
leakage \citep{kapoor2023leakage} and site or demographic shortcuts
\citep{zech2018confounding,brown2012adhd200} are well documented.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
% ===========================================================================================

\paragraph{Loop.} A fixed harness loads the data and defines labels, splits and metric; it is never
shown to or edited by the LLM. A solution is one Python file implementing
\texttt{featurize(items, ctx)}, which never receives labels, and
\texttt{fit\_predict(F\_train, y\_train, F\_test, ctx)}, which receives training labels only. Each
round the LLM (Claude Opus 5.5, reasoning effort \emph{high}) receives a general brief, a project
brief, the current best solution and the full history of previous rounds (rationale, declared
feature/model families, score, kept or not, last failure), and returns a complete new solution.
Candidates run on CPU containers with a 1{,}500\,s limit (timeouts and crashes count as failed
rounds). A loop runs 20 rounds with patience 10 and keeps a candidate iff its development score
improves by more than $10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Tasks.} \Cref{tab:tasks} lists the 12 tasks: abnormal EEG (TUAB \citep{obeid2016tuh});
depression and ADHD (TDBRAIN \citep{vandijk2022tdbrain}); schizophrenia (ASEEG \citep{aseeg});
per-second neonatal seizure detection (Helsinki \citep{stevenson2019helsinki}); autism and ADHD
from fMRI connectivity (ABIDE I \citep{dimartino2014abide}, ADHD-200; \citep{pcp}); colorectal
cancer (CRC) and IBD from 16S profiles (MicrobiomeHD \citep{duvallet2017,baxter2016}); Alzheimer's
disease (AD) from plasma \citep{pxd065835} and CSF \citep{bader2020} proteomics; and drug response
from CCLE expression to PRISM AUC \citep{corsello2020,depmap24q4}. Seeds are deliberately standard:
log band power with L2 logistic regression (scalp EEG); line length, RMS and band power with
gradient boosting and smoothing (neonatal EEG); Fisher-$z$ CC200 connectivity with logistic
regression (fMRI); genus abundance with a random forest (16S); log$_2$, median centring and
logistic regression (proteomics); and ridge regression on the most variable genes (drug response).

\paragraph{Shortcut-free protocol (v2).} Our first protocol (v1) passed age, sex, head motion and
site to the solution and scored multi-site tasks with pooled AUROC; the loop used them
(\cref{sec:shortcuts}). All other results use v2: no demographics or motion; site, study and
batch only for harmonization; multi-site tasks scored \emph{within site} (size-weighted mean of
per-site AUROCs); and TDBRAIN tasks restricted to the age range shared by patients and controls.
v2 was designed after we saw the v1 results.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} For each split seed (1, 2, 3), 25\% of subjects (grouped by subject;
stratified by label and site) form a held-out \emph{Eval} set and the rest a \emph{dev pool}. The
loop optimizes \emph{Dev}, a 5-fold grouped CV score inside the dev pool. After the loop, seed and
champion are refit on the whole dev pool and scored \emph{once} on Eval and on external datasets.
The three Eval sets overlap, so splits are not independent replicates.

\paragraph{Same-budget baselines.} All arms start from the same seed, use the same harness, time
limit and 20 Dev evaluations, keep a candidate only if it beats the seed on Dev, and are scored once
on the same Eval sets. \emph{One-shot}: one LLM proposal. \emph{Best-of-20}: 20 independent LLM
proposals that see the briefs and the seed but no history or scores, isolating the value of feedback.
\emph{Optuna}: 20 trials of multivariate TPE over a hand-written parameterized pipeline per project
(which feature blocks to include, which classifier, its regularization). These search spaces were
written \emph{after} the main study and include feature families the loop had used, which makes
this baseline conservative from the loop's point of view.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Per task, the gain is the mean over three splits; its CI and two-sided $p$
come from a paired bootstrap that resamples subjects from the union of the Eval sets (2{,}000 draws,
$(b+1)/(B+1)$ per tail \citep{davison1997bootstrap}), Holm-adjusted across 12 tasks
\citep{holm1979}. This test treats the fitted models as fixed; it does not capture variation between
splits or loop runs, which we report separately. Pooled over the 11 AUROC tasks, we report the
equal-weight mean of task gains with a $t$-interval (primary) and DerSimonian--Laird random effects
\citep{dersimonian1986} (secondary). Run-to-run repeats, shortcut-only models and the transduction
audit are described in \cref{app:methods}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================================

\subsection{Development gains are guaranteed; held-out gains are not}\label{sec:dev-eval}
Across 36 runs the loop ran 710 rounds and kept 283 candidates (10 failed rounds). Dev improved in
36/36 runs, as the keep-if-better rule guarantees; Eval improved in 27/36, with per-split
$p<0.05$ in 15/36 (\cref{tab:main-app}). The mean Dev gain (0.072) exceeded the mean Eval gain
(0.049) and the two were only moderately correlated ($r=0.38$; \cref{fig:dev-eval}). CRC, plasma AD
and ABIDE autism lost or stayed flat on Eval despite consistent Dev gains (\cref{tab:combined}).

\subsection{Feedback, not the LLM alone, produces the gains}\label{sec:baselines-results}
\Cref{tab:combined} and \cref{fig:baselines} compare the loop with the baselines. One LLM proposal
was kept in 15/36 splits and changed Eval by $+0.002$ on average. Twenty proposals without feedback
did better ($+0.014$) but were kept in only 25/36 splits: on neonatal seizure detection and ADHD
(TDBRAIN), none of 120 independent proposals beat the seed on Dev, while the loop improved every
split of both tasks. The loop's own first two rounds on neonatal seizures also failed in every split;
improvement came once it could build on what had failed. All 720 best-of-20 proposals ran, so low
adoption reflects proposal quality rather than a broken baseline.

Optuna gained $+0.035$ on average and matched the loop on both TDBRAIN tasks (MDD 0.946 vs.\ 0.954;
ADHD 0.918 vs.\ 0.922) and on ADHD-200 (0.650 vs.\ 0.665); it beat the loop on plasma and CSF AD.
The loop's advantage over Optuna was Holm-significant only on abnormal EEG ($+0.029$) and neonatal
seizure detection ($+0.075$).

\subsection{Corrected and pooled statistics}\label{sec:stats-results}
Against the seed, the loop's gain is Holm-significant on five tasks and its loss on CRC
(\cref{tab:combined}). The ADHD-200 gain rests on one split (a $t$-test across splits gives
$p=0.39$), which the subject bootstrap does not capture. Pooled over 11 AUROC tasks
(\cref{tab:pooled}), the loop gained $+0.052$ over the seed ($p=0.023$; $I^2=85\%$), $+0.038$ over
best-of-20 ($p=0.051$) and $+0.017$ over Optuna ($p=0.29$). The gain over the seed depends on the
TDBRAIN tasks, whose signal does not transfer (\cref{sec:transfer}): without them it is $+0.037$
($p=0.11$), and $+0.030$ ($p=0.21$) without ADHD-200 as well. Per-task results for all comparisons
are in \cref{tab:stats} and \cref{fig:meta}.

\subsection{The loop finds shortcuts when they exist}\label{sec:shortcuts}
Under protocol v1, every ADHD-200, ABIDE and pooled-IBD champion referenced non-biological inputs:
site, age and sex (ADHD-200); site, age, sex and head motion (ABIDE); study identity (IBD). These
inputs carry substantial signal alone (\cref{fig:shortcuts}): site plus age and sex reach a pooled
Eval AUROC of 0.775 on ADHD-200, close to the v1 champion (0.813); study identity alone gives 0.691
on IBD. Rescored within site, the v1 ADHD-200 champion drops from 0.813 to 0.731, close to the v2
champion (0.717). Within-site scoring removes the site shortcut but not the demographic one: on
ADHD-200, age and sex alone give a within-site AUROC of 0.647, just below the v2 champion's 0.665.
Removing demographic \emph{inputs} does not stop a model from using demographic \emph{signal}
encoded in the imaging data.

\subsection{In-distribution gains did not transfer to other cohorts}\label{sec:transfer}
Applied unchanged to external data (\cref{fig:transfer}, \cref{tab:external}), only abnormal EEG
transferred: on the official TUAB evaluation split the champion beat the seed in all three splits
(AUROC 0.867 $\to$ 0.928, all CIs excluding zero). That split is from the same hospital, so it is a
near-in-distribution check. The TDBRAIN depression seed was \emph{inverted} on the Mumtaz cohort
(AUROC 0.25--0.29) and the champions reached only 0.28--0.62. The numerical gain moves an inverted
model towards chance, which points to dataset-specific case/control signal rather than a
transferable marker. The schizophrenia champion was worse than the seed on the MSU cohort in all
three splits (0.696 $\to$ 0.538) and mixed on RepOD; the CRC champion lost on all four external
cohorts on average.

\subsection{Variance and transduction}\label{sec:variance}
Repeating the loop on the same split varied the Eval gain less than drawing a new split
(run-to-run SD median 0.016 vs.\ split-to-split 0.042, smaller for 6 of 7 tasks; \cref{fig:variance}).
The sign was stable on IBD, MDD and schizophrenia and flipped between repeats on CRC, CSF AD and drug
response; repeats were not affordable for abnormal EEG or neonatal seizure. All seeds were exactly
inductive; scoring Eval inside a reference batch built from the dev pool changed champion scores by
$+0.001$ on average (worst $-0.024$), so reported scores do not exploit Eval-set statistics
(\cref{fig:audit}). Eleven champions output batch-relative ranks and would need a reference cohort
to score single patients.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================================
\paragraph{When the loop helped.} The clearest gains came on abnormal EEG and neonatal seizure
detection: signal-processing tasks with large or well-annotated data and clinically meaningful
labels. They were the only tasks where the loop beat a loop-informed Optuna search, and on both,
independent LLM proposals almost never beat the seed. The active ingredient is feedback, not the
LLM's prior knowledge alone.

\paragraph{When it did not.} Elsewhere the loop was not reliably better than ordinary search, and
on three tasks it was worse than the seed on held-out data despite consistent Dev gains. Its
largest in-distribution gains, on TDBRAIN, were matched by Optuna and did not transfer, pointing to
dataset-specific signal that any optimizer would find. Given site and demographics, the loop used
them. A held-out set scored once, a non-LLM baseline, shortcut-only models and an external cohort
each changed our reading of at least one task; the Dev curve alone would have changed none. Our
numbers are also not better than published results where a rough comparison is possible (Helsinki
0.899 vs.\ about 0.955 \citep{tapani2022}; on CRC the seed already matched the MicrobiomeHD baseline
\citep{duvallet2017}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} Several Eval sets are small (20--43 subjects), the three splits overlap,
and repeats cover only 7 of 12 tasks. We used one LLM at one setting and a CPU budget that favours
light models. The Optuna spaces were written with knowledge of the loop's results. TDBRAIN cases and
controls were recorded separately, and ADHD-200 groups differ in sex and age within sites. Protocol
v2 was designed after seeing v1. Further dataset caveats are in \cref{app:caveats}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
An LLM autoresearch loop with feedback improved held-out performance over a simple seed on most of
12 biomedical tasks, clearly more than the same LLM without feedback, but significantly more than a
tuned non-LLM search on only two signal-processing tasks. The same loop exploited site and
demographic shortcuts when allowed, and its psychiatric-EEG gains did not transfer. Gains from such
a loop are a hypothesis to test on held-out, shortcut-free and external data, not evidence of a
better biomarker.

\section{Additional methods}\label{app:methods}
\paragraph{Prompt and rules.} The brief asks for one well-motivated change per round, forbids reading
anything outside what the harness passes in, requires inner CV (grouped by subject) for any tuning
and lists the available libraries (numpy, scipy, scikit-learn, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, MNE,
pyRiemann, nilearn, PyTorch on CPU, among others). Candidates run on Modal containers with 2--16
cores depending on the task.

\paragraph{Run-to-run variance.} For the seven cheapest tasks, split 1 was rerun twice more from
scratch (same seed, Dev CV and Eval set; only the LLM's sampling differs).

\paragraph{Shortcut-only models.} On the four multi-site tasks, L2 logistic regressions using only site
or study one-hot codes and/or demographics were fit on the dev pool and scored on Eval with both
the pooled (v1) and within-site (v2) metric.

\paragraph{Transduction audit.} The Eval protocol featurizes dev-pool and Eval items in one call and
passes the whole Eval batch to \texttt{fit\_predict}. For every seed and champion we tested (i)
whether dev-pool features change when Eval items are featurized with them; (ii) a \emph{chunked}
score, with Eval split into 20 chunks (10 for large tasks), each featurized with the dev pool and
predicted on its own; and (iii) a \emph{reference-batch} score, as (ii) but each chunk predicted
inside a batch of dev pool plus chunk, so batch statistics come from training-time data.

\paragraph{Pooled statistics.} Drug response (Pearson $r$) is excluded from pooling. For random
effects, task standard errors are floored at 0.005. Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted values
\citep{benjamini1995} are in the released files.

\section{Dataset caveats}\label{app:caveats}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TDBRAIN.} All controls are adults, so tasks are age-matched to the shared range (ADHD:
        adults only); age is never given to the model. Patients and controls were recruited and
        recorded separately, so state or protocol differences cannot be excluded.
  \item \textbf{ASEEG.} The channel order is undocumented and was inferred from data
        (its correlation structure matches labelled RepOD recordings: $r=0.855$ for the
        standard 10--20 order, $r<0.1$ for the alternatives). MSU is adolescents with 1-min, 16-channel recordings.
  \item \textbf{TUAB.} First 60\,s skipped, next 5\,min used, 19 channels at 100\,Hz, clipped at
        $\pm$800\,$\mu$V \citep{gemein2020}.
  \item \textbf{Helsinki.} Label = majority of three experts per second; published results often use
        leave-one-out or other annotation rules \citep{tapani2022,hogan2025}.
  \item \textbf{ABIDE / ADHD-200.} Within-site AUROC is stricter than the pooled metrics usually
        reported \citep{abraham2017,heinsfeld2018}.
  \item \textbf{Proteomics.} Plasma: single centre, the only open labelled AD plasma MS set we found.
        CSF label = clinical diagnosis.
  \item \textbf{Drug response.} 200 most variable drugs ($\geq$70\% coverage) from the PRISM
        secondary screen; per-drug Pearson $r$ on held-out cell lines.
\end{itemize}

Champions typically extended the seed's representation with domain features: finer clinical
sub-bands, aperiodic slope, connectivity, complexity and Riemannian covariance features (EEG);
per-neonate and per-channel background normalization and multi-scale temporal context (neonatal
seizures); log-ratio transforms and batch correction (microbiome); known AD proteins and pathway
aggregates (proteomics). Ensembles of a linear model with boosting or kernel methods were common.
